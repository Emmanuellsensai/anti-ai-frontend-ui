# Plain copy

Copy is the fastest tell. Generated copy has a rhythm: a short claim, a comma, a contrast. Write the way a person
on the team would explain the product to a colleague: what it is, what it does, one fact that proves it.

## Rules

1. **State what it is.** The H1 says what the product is or does, in the words a customer would use.
2. **Facts over adjectives.** "Loads in 0.8 s on a mid-range phone" beats "blazing fast".
3. **Numbers exactly as measured,** with the method in one small line and the unflattering numbers left in.
4. **One idea per sentence.** Short sentences are fine; slogans are not.
5. **No second-person hype.** Do not tell the reader how they will feel.
6. **Headings are labels or plain statements,** not posters: "How we train it", "What works now, and what is next".
7. **Buttons are verbs:** "Try it", "See the numbers". Not "Get started today!".
8. **Read it aloud.** If you would not say it across a table, rewrite it.
9. **Say what it does not do yet.** A short honest "next" list is more convincing than a promise.
10. **Never put private details in public copy** (keys, internal hostnames, unreleased names, customer data).

## One clear message

A landing page says one thing, in the hero, in a sentence a customer would repeat ("Send an invoice in under a minute.
We chase late payers for you."). Every section after it supports that sentence: how it works, what it does, what it costs,
proof, how to start, questions. Integration details, API lists, stacks and long timelines go on a linked page. If a
redesign brief says "keep all the information", keep it reachable, not all on the home page; ask if unsure.

## Facts come from the source of truth, never from the old page

Before stating what a product does (languages, integrations, prices, status), check the current source: the code
that does it, the status doc, the owner. Old marketing pages go stale; copying their claims ("coming soon" on features
that had already shipped) puts a wrong statement on a new page. Use real lines from the product (its own script,
its own responses) instead of writing sample copy.

## Words and shapes to avoid

- "X, built for Y." / "Built for the phone line, not just the lab." / "not just X, but Y"
- "X, actually." / "what it actually sounds like" / "the line you actually have"
- "Working today. Growing every week." / "Always improving." / "Getting better every day."
- "earns its place", "end to end", "seamless", "effortless", "unlock", "supercharge", "elevate", "empower",
  "next-generation", "cutting-edge", "game-changing", "revolutionise", "AI-powered" as a headline.
- Three-part rhythm lists: "Fast, simple, secure."
- Rhetorical questions as headings: "Ready to transform your workflow?"
- Exclamation marks. Em-dash-heavy sentences. Parenthetical cleverness.
- "Whether you are X or Y".
- A tagline that could sit on any company's site.

## Section copy checklist

- Eyebrow: two or three words, plain ("How it works", "Results", "Who we are").
- Headline: a statement or a label. Under about 12 words.
- Lead paragraph: two sentences at most. The second carries a fact.
- Captions and notes: state method and caveats ("tested on 200 real orders, on a mid-range Android phone").
- FAQ: real questions people ask, answered in plain sentences with real numbers and a contact address if the answer is "not yet".
